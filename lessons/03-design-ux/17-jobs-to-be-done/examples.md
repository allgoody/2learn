## Example 1 — From an interview note to a job statement

The note below came back from a research call. Most of it describes the person rather than the situation, so nothing says when a product becomes relevant. Pull the trigger, the first alternative and the obstacle, then write one job statement that names no product.

```text
Priya, 34, marketing manager, organised, dislikes admin. Says she would
use a tool that automates invoicing. Prefers mobile. Would pay for
something that saves time. Sometimes uses a spreadsheet a colleague made.
```

### Solution

```text
Trigger:      the second time this month she re-did an invoice by hand
Tried first:  the colleague's spreadsheet, then a call to accounts
Almost stopped: sign-up wanted a company number she did not have yet

Job statement: When a monthly invoice has to be redone by hand, I want to
send it from the record I already have, so I can close the month without
chasing accounts.
```

The original would fit thousands of people and predicts nothing: age, title and stated preference rarely decide behaviour. The statement keeps the situation and the progress, and names no product, so a stranger could recognise the switch. The usual mistake is writing "I want an invoicing tool", which turns the statement into a feature and rules out the simpler alternatives.

## Example 2 — The table that includes doing nothing

The team tracks two rival invoicing apps and calls them the competition. For the same job, write a hire-and-fire table that adds the manual alternatives and says why people fire each one.

```text
Hires: App A, App B
Fires: too expensive, too complicated
```

### Solution

```text
Hires                          Fires, and why
accounting suite               asks for a company number at sign-up
mobile invoicing app           one invoice at a time on a phone
colleague's spreadsheet         breaks the week she leaves
reminder on the fridge         no record of what was sent
waiting until month end        the handover is re-done only once
```

Non-consumption is where the job's real competition sits: the spreadsheet and the fridge note are kept because they are familiar and never ask for anything. A table of named products hides the effort of switching, so the team optimises inside the wrong set. The usual mistake is treating the incumbent as a straw man instead of writing down what it does well.
