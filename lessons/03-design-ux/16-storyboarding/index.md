## What it is

A storyboard sketches a user's sequence of steps as a short comic: a row of panels, each with a caption saying what the person is doing and thinking. It is drawn before the interface exists, and its subject is the person, not the screen.

## Why it matters

A flow diagram shows boxes and arrows; nobody can tell from it whether the third step is reasonable, or where the person is standing, or what happens when the phone rings. A storyboard makes the sequence concrete enough for a group to disagree with — the cheapest moment to discover that the step everyone agreed on does not fit the situation. It is also the fastest way to show a non-designer, an executive or a client what a change actually means for someone's day.

## Core ideas

### Draw the person, not the screen

Keep the human in every panel. The interface can be a rectangle in their hand; the interesting content is where they are, what they are holding and what interrupts them.

```text
Panel 1  Picker at the end of an aisle, terminal two aisles away.
Panel 2  Checks the clipboard. Hands full of two boxes.
Panel 3  Phone in pocket, gloves on — cannot type.
Panel 4  Walks back to the terminal again. 40 seconds gone.
```

The problem is visible in panel 3 without a word of explanation.

### One panel per step, in the user's order

Panels follow the person's timeline, not the system's. If the person repeats a step, draw it twice — the repetition is the finding.

### Caption what changes, not what is drawn

Each caption states the user's goal or the obstacle at that moment. Avoid narrating the drawing ("a man stands in a warehouse").

### Six to eight panels is the useful length

Fewer and the sequence is vague; more and the group stops reading. Split into two storyboards rather than extending one.

### Draw it with the group

Storyboarding works best as an activity, not a deliverable: give everyone a pen and four panels, then compare. Disagreements about the picture are disagreements about the flow, and that is exactly the argument worth having early.

## Practice

### Storyboard one broken routine

Choose a task you have watched someone perform. Draw six panels of what actually happens, captions in the user's words, including the interruption or workaround that shows the problem.

Done when: someone who has never seen the product can point at the panel where the task goes wrong.

### Storyboard a proposed change

Draw the same six panels again with your change in place. Keep the person and the surroundings identical so the only difference is the design decision.

Done when: the before and after boards differ in exactly the steps you intended to change and nowhere else.

## Common mistakes

- **Storyboarding the interface instead of the person** — the board becomes a low-fidelity screen flow and the context disappears; fix: draw where they are standing and what they are holding.
- **Labels instead of captions** — "Screen 2" says nothing about the user's goal; fix: write what the person is trying to do or what stopped them.
- **Twenty panels** — the group stops reading and the detail hides the problem; fix: cut to six or eight, split if you need more.
- **Polished drawing** — effort in the artwork discourages revision; fix: keep it to stick figures and rectangles, drawn in one sitting.
- **Drawing it alone and presenting it** — the group treats it as a finished decision and argues about the drawing; fix: hand out pens and draw it together.
