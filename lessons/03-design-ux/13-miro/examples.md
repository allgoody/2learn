## Example 1 — A board built before anyone joins

A 90-minute workshop starts on an empty board, and the first ten minutes go on orientation. Turn the agenda below into prepared frames so a participant can open the board and know where to type.

```text
Agenda
0:00  prompt + decision statement
0:15  evidence wall
0:35  six sketch areas, one per group
1:05  dot voting
1:20  parking lot
```

### Solution

```text
Frame 1  Prompt and the decision statement
Frame 2  Evidence wall (five quotes, pre-placed)
Frame 3  Six sketch areas, one per group
Frame 4  Dot voting grid, anonymous
Frame 5  Parking lot
```

Each timed block now has a frame, a prompt and a place to respond, so the group spends the session on the work rather than finding the toolbar. Starting empty is the usual mistake: it costs the group its first ten minutes and the first idea is lost while everyone orients.

## Example 2 — Closing the board with owned actions

The session ends with a wall of green stickies and no decision. Turn the chosen items into an owned list, then say what happens to the board itself.

```text
Board: 24 notes, 6 with the most dots, no owners
Team:  "great session"
After: board stays open, everyone keeps editing
```

### Solution

```text
Decided (owner, date)
  Move help to the header        Priya, 12 Jun
  Cut the third sign-up step     Tom, 19 Jun
  Test labels with 5 people      Priya, 26 Jun
Then: copy these into the team's document tool and archive
      the board read-only.
```

Attaching a name and a date to every chosen item turns a vote into a decision, and closing the board stops it becoming a second, staler source of truth. Leaving it open is the common failure: people keep editing after the decision, so the record no longer says what was agreed.
