## What it is

Time management is estimating how long work takes, choosing what to do first against a stated priority, and protecting blocks of uninterrupted time for work that needs concentration. The artefact is a plan you can compare against what actually happened.

## Why it matters

Technical work is not interchangeable per hour. A meeting interrupts a debugging session in a way that costs the twenty minutes of re-establishing context as well as the meeting itself, which is why a fragmented day produces far less than the sum of its free hours. The other half is estimation: without recorded estimates, a team cannot tell a slow week from a wrong estimate, and the same over-commitment repeats. Both halves are learnable habits with numbers attached.

## Core ideas

### Estimate in blocks, then compare

Give each task a rough size — half a day, a day, three days — and record what it actually took. The correction factor for your own estimates appears within a few weeks and is usually stable.

```text
Estimated 1d   ->  actual 2d    (unknown library)
Estimated 1d   ->  actual 1d
Estimated 3d   ->  actual 4.5d  (integration surprises)
Pattern: multiply by 1.5, and never plan a day full of 1d items
```

### Protect blocks, not minutes

Two or three uninterrupted blocks of ninety minutes beat a day of fifteen-minute gaps. Put them in the calendar as appointments and defend them like meetings.

### Batch the shallow work

Email, messages, reviews and small questions are real work but they fragment attention. Give them two or three fixed windows a day and stay out of the inbox between them.

### Make notifications default to off, and pull on a schedule

A notification is someone else choosing your attention. Turn off everything that is not a genuine page, and check the channels at your own cadence.

### Say no with a trade, not a refusal

"I can do that this week if the migration slips" puts the decision where it belongs and keeps the relationship. A bare no invites escalation; a trade invites prioritisation.

### Leave slack, and treat it as part of the plan

A day planned at a hundred per cent has no capacity for an incident, and incidents are certain. Plan four fifths of the available time and the schedule survives contact.

## Practice

### Track estimates against actuals for five tasks

For five tasks, write the estimate before starting, then the actual, then the ratio.

Done when: you have an average ratio for your own estimates and have applied it to the next plan you wrote.

### Defend one two-hour block for a week

Book one two-hour block each day for five days, with notifications off and a single task named in advance.

Done when: at least four of the five blocks happened, and you can name what you finished in them.

## Common mistakes

- **Planning a day full of one-day items** — one surprise destroys the plan; fix: plan four fifths of the time and keep a correction factor.
- **Notification-driven work** — someone else chooses your attention all day; fix: batch the shallow channels into fixed windows.
- **Refusing without a trade** — the request escalates instead of being prioritised; fix: offer the exchange explicitly.
- **No uninterrupted block** — concentrated work never gets a chance to start; fix: book blocks in advance and defend them.
- **Estimates never compared to actuals** — the same over-commitment repeats; fix: record both and compute your own ratio.
