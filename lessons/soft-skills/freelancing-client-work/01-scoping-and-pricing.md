## What it is

Scoping is writing down what the client gets, what they do not, and what has to be true for the work to finish — including what you need from them. Pricing is choosing a number for that scope, with the assumptions that produced it stated so that a change of scope has an obvious cost.

## Why it matters

Scope creep is not usually a client behaving badly; it is an unwritten boundary meeting an ordinary conversation. Each request is small and reasonable in isolation, and together they turn a profitable project into unpaid work. A written scope with a stated change process makes every addition a decision with a price, which almost always reduces the volume of additions as well as the resentment.

## Core ideas

### Write inclusions and exclusions in the same list

The exclusions are the load-bearing half. Say what is not included, specifically, in the same document and the same tone.

```text
Included   up to 5 pages, responsive layout, contact form with email
           delivery, one round of revisions
Not included  copywriting, photography, hosting setup, analytics,
           email marketing integration, content beyond 5 pages
Client provides  final text and images before the start date
```

The line about what the client provides prevents the commonest cause of a slipped schedule.

### Price from the estimate, then add a margin for what you know you miss

Use your own correction factor from past projects and say the assumptions out loud. A fixed price built on an unexamined estimate is a bet you will lose quietly.

```text
Estimate      3 weeks at the day rate
Correction    x1.4 from the last comparable project -> 4.2 weeks
Fixed price   priced on 4 weeks, valid for 30 days
Assumption    the client's design system exists and is current
```

### Separate the unknown from the known in the price

Where something genuinely cannot be estimated — an undocumented integration, a legacy system — offer a paid discovery phase first, and make the build price conditional on its outcome. This is far better than a contingency you cannot defend.

### Put an expiry on the quote

A price with no date drifts into a commitment you made about a different world. Thirty days is normal and easy to defend.

### Charge for changes, and make that normal from the start

Write the change process into the proposal: a written request, an estimate, an approval before work starts. The first change is the moment the process either exists or does not.

## Practice

### Write a scope for a real project

Take a project you have done or are about to do and write inclusions, exclusions and client responsibilities as three lists.

Done when: two exclusions are things a client would plausibly expect, and each would be a priced change rather than an argument.

### Price it from your own correction factor

Estimate the work, apply your historical ratio, add the margin, and write the assumptions and the validity date under the number.

Done when: the price has at least two written assumptions and an expiry date, and you would defend it out loud without apologising.

## Common mistakes

- **Exclusions left unsaid** — the boundary is negotiated one small request at a time; fix: write the exclusions in the proposal.
- **Fixed price from an unadjusted estimate** — the project is under water before it starts; fix: apply your own correction factor and state the assumptions.
- **No client responsibilities** — the schedule slips waiting for content and it looks like your delay; fix: list what the client provides and by when.
- **Quotes with no expiry** — an old number becomes this year's commitment; fix: date the proposal.
- **No change process** — the first small addition sets the precedent that additions are free; fix: price the first change.
